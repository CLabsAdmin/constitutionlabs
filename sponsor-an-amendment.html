Amendment Lab

How to Sponsor an Amendment

Two ways to bring an amendment to the Lab, depending on how far along your thinking is.

Still in the idea stage?

If there’s an amendment you’d like us to see but you haven’t yet drafted or developed it, share the idea and we’ll respond within a few days with some initial feedback.

Further along?

If you’ve moved past the idea stage — you’ve drafted your amendment, done some research, and built some early support — it may be a good candidate for the Amendment Lab.

If our screening committee determines it meets our criteria, we’ll work with you at no cost to analyze and refine it. You’ll be paired with a team of volunteers whose expertise spans law, data analysis, research, and public policy, and who will help you identify the strengths to build on and the vulnerabilities to address.

Start by reviewing the criteria for acceptance. When you’re ready, submit your request and we’ll get back to you shortly with a decision and next steps. (Our capacity is sometimes limited, so we may not be able to take on every request that meets the criteria.)

Criteria for Lab Support

What we look for.

The Amendment Lab helps design amendments that meet all three of these criteria:

A. Structural focus

Structural questions relate more to HOW government works — how it is structured, how power is distributed, checked, and held accountable, and where political independence would benefit all. We might have disagreements about WHICH structure, but a structural amendment works the same way regardless of which party holds office or which side of a debate you favor. Policy questions relate to WHAT government should decide — the priorities it sets on money, regulation, and resources, and where it lands on contested questions of rights and values.

In scope — structural questions like how elections are financed, how district lines are drawn, how long officials serve and how they’re appointed, how the branches check one another, how the government borrows and spends, and how officials are held to ethical standards.
Out of scope — policy and values questions such as abortion, gun ownership, education, immigration, religion, symbolism, culture, morality, the size of government, or taxation. These ask what the government should decide. They matter enormously — but Americans settle them through politics, legislation, and the ballot box, and a constitutional structural fix is the wrong instrument for them. Staying neutral on policy is also what lets people across the spectrum trust the Lab’s work.

A quick test: a structural amendment sets a fair rule of the game that holds no matter who’s in power — even if the parties fight over it. If an amendment only makes sense when your side wins the underlying issue, it’s probably policy.

B. Organizational sponsorship

The amendment is sponsored by an organization, group, or coalition with a track record of constructive civic engagement. If you’re working on your own without that kind of backing, you most likely have an idea rather than a Lab-ready project and should start by clicking Share an Idea above.

C. Evidence of appeal

There is some evidence of broad appeal among American citizens — either general (for example, credible issue-specific research) or specific to your amendment (for example, a petition signed by hundreds or thousands of supporters).
