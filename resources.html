How Article V Works

From idea to ratification.

The Constitution spells out precisely how it can be changed — two ways to propose an amendment, two ways to ratify one, and a deliberately high bar at every step.

1

Proposal. An amendment can be proposed either by a two-thirds vote of both houses of Congress, or by a convention called by Congress when two-thirds of the state legislatures (34 states) request one. Every amendment so far has taken the congressional route; the convention path has never been carried through to completion.

2

Ratification. A proposed amendment takes effect only when three-quarters of the states (38 of 50) ratify it — either through their legislatures or through special state ratifying conventions, as Congress directs. Just one amendment, the 21st, was ratified by state conventions rather than legislatures.

3

Certification. Once the 38th state ratifies, the Archivist of the United States certifies that the amendment has been adopted and publishes it as part of the Constitution. There is no presidential signature and no role for the courts — the President plays no formal part in Article V.

Congress sometimes attaches a deadline for ratification (often seven years) and sometimes does not. The 27th Amendment shows how wide that window can be: it was proposed in 1789 and not ratified until 1992 — 202 years later.

Freedoms of Religion, Speech & Press · 1791
Protects freedom of religion, speech, the press, assembly, and the right to petition the government.
2
Right to Bear Arms · 1791
Protects the right of the people to keep and bear arms.
3
Quartering of Soldiers · 1791
Bars the government from housing soldiers in private homes without the owner’s consent.
4
Search & Seizure · 1791
Protects against unreasonable searches and seizures and sets requirements for warrants.
5
Rights of the Accused · 1791
Guarantees due process, protection against self-incrimination and double jeopardy, and just compensation for property taken.
6
Fair Trial · 1791
Guarantees a speedy, public trial by jury, and the rights to counsel and to confront witnesses.
7
Civil Jury Trials · 1791
Preserves the right to a jury trial in many civil cases.
8
No Cruel Punishment · 1791
Prohibits excessive bail and fines and cruel and unusual punishments.
9
Unenumerated Rights · 1791
Affirms that people hold rights beyond those specifically listed in the Constitution.
10
States’ Reserved Powers · 1791
Reserves to the states or the people any powers not delegated to the federal government.
11
Suits Against States · 1795
Limits the ability to sue a state in federal court, establishing a form of state sovereign immunity.
12
Electoral College · 1804
Revised presidential elections so electors cast separate votes for President and Vice President.
13
Abolition of Slavery · 1865
Abolished slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for a crime.
14
Citizenship & Equal Protection · 1868
Defines citizenship and guarantees due process and equal protection of the laws.
15
Voting Rights & Race · 1870
Bars denying the vote based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude.
16
Federal Income Tax · 1913
Gives Congress the power to levy an income tax without apportioning it among the states.
17
Direct Election of Senators · 1913
Provides for U.S. Senators to be elected directly by the people rather than by state legislatures.
18
Prohibition · 1919
Banned the manufacture, sale, and transport of alcoholic beverages. Later repealed by the 21st Amendment.
19
Women’s Suffrage · 1920
Bars denying the vote on the basis of sex, extending suffrage to women nationwide.
20
Terms & Transitions · 1933
Sets the start dates for presidential and congressional terms, reducing the “lame-duck” period.
21
Repeal of Prohibition · 1933
Repealed the 18th Amendment — the only amendment to undo an earlier one, and the only one ratified by state conventions.
22
Presidential Term Limits · 1951
Limits a President to two elected terms.
23
D.C. Electoral Votes · 1961
Grants the District of Columbia electors in the Electoral College.
24
Abolition of Poll Taxes · 1964
Prohibits poll taxes as a condition of voting in federal elections.
25
Presidential Succession · 1967
Clarifies presidential succession and procedures for a President unable to serve.
26
Voting Age of 18 · 1971
Lowers the voting age to 18 in all elections.
27
Congressional Pay · 1992
Delays any change in congressional pay until after the next election of Representatives.

Historical Constitution Resources

Where to dig deeper.

The institutions that hold the primary sources and the leading academic scholarship — and the organizations that teach it.

Where to find original documents, credible scholarship, and trustworthy teaching about the Constitution. Most of these are strictly nonpartisan; two widely used curriculum providers are flagged for a mild lean, using the key above.

Official records & primary sources

★ National Archives — America’s Founding Documents

Holds the original engrossed Constitution, Declaration, and Bill of Rights, with high-resolution images and full transcripts.

★ Constitution Annotated (Library of Congress)

The official, continuously updated clause-by-clause analysis of the Constitution as interpreted by the Supreme Court.

★ Founders Online (National Archives)

Searchable correspondence and papers of Washington, Franklin, Adams, Jefferson, Madison, and Hamilton.

★ The Avalon Project (Yale Law School)

A free library of founding-era legal and historical documents, including the Constitution, the Federalist, and the ratification debates.

★ The Founders’ Constitution (University of Chicago Press)

A canonical, clause-by-clause anthology of the writings that shaped the Framers’ thinking — called “the Oxford English Dictionary of American constitutional history.”

★ Center for the Study of the American Constitution (UW–Madison)

Home of the Documentary History of the Ratification of the Constitution, the definitive primary-source record of ratification — free online and cited by the Supreme Court.

★ ConSource — The Constitutional Sources Project

A free, fully-indexed digital library of constitutional source documents, now stewarded by the Quill Project at the University of Oxford.

★ The Amendments Project (Harvard)

A searchable archive of the full text of nearly every amendment proposed to the Constitution since 1789 — more than 11,000, including thousands that failed. Its accompanying essays reflect the perspective of its director, historian Jill Lepore.

Education & museums

★ National Constitution Center

Congressionally chartered and explicitly nonpartisan; home of the Interactive Constitution, which pairs liberal and conservative scholars on every clause, plus Constitution 101 and the Founders’ Library.

★ iCivics

Founded by Justice Sandra Day O’Connor; the nation’s leading nonpartisan civic-education nonprofit, with free games, lessons, and curricula.

★ Annenberg Classroom

Free, nonpartisan constitutional and civics resources, including well-produced documentary videos on each amendment and major case.

★ Gilder Lehrman Institute of American History

Nonpartisan American-history education with a deep collection of founding-era primary sources and teacher resources.

★ Bill of Rights Institute

Widely used classroom resources on the founding documents. A Koch-network founding and a free-market, limited-government emphasis give it a mild conservative lean, though its materials are presented as neutral.

★ Teach Democracy (formerly Constitutional Rights Foundation)

A 60-year-old civic-education nonprofit behind Bill of Rights in Action and a national Mock Trial program. Its rights-based, “action civics” approach and funding give it a mild liberal lean, though it describes itself as nonpartisan.

Comparative & specialized

★ Constitute

The Comparative Constitutions Project’s tool to read, search, and compare the world’s constitutions by topic — useful for seeing how other nations structure and amend their charters.

★ Congressional Research Service

Congress’s nonpartisan research arm; its public reports include a regularly updated fact sheet on proposals to amend the Constitution.

★ Federal Judicial Center

The federal judiciary’s research and education agency; its History of the Federal Judiciary covers landmark cases, judicial biographies, and the evolution of the courts.

Foundations & Think Tanks

Who studies structural reform.

Policy organizations that research and recommend changes to how government is structured are shown below, each identified with their political philosophy lean. To be included, organizations needed to demonstrate intellectual rigor and self-critiquing scholarship in their work.

★★ American Constitution Society

The progressive legal network, and the principal counterpart to the Federalist Society.

★★ Brennan Center for Justice (NYU)

Research and litigation on democracy, voting rights, and money in politics; widely cited in courts and Congress.

★ Brookings Institution

The oldest U.S. think tank; broad governance scholarship with a centrist-to-center-left cast and gold-standard sourcing.

★ Constitutional Accountability Center

A think tank and law firm advancing text-and-history arguments for progressive constitutional outcomes.

★ Bipartisan Policy Center

Founded by former Senate leaders of both parties; governance and structural-reform work that is bipartisan by design.

★ American Enterprise Institute

Center-right scholarship on governance, economics, and constitutional questions; rigorous and widely cited.

★ Cato Institute

Libertarian; deep constitutional and civil-liberties work, including the annual Cato Supreme Court Review, with an independent streak.

★ Georgetown Center for the Constitution

Georgetown Law’s academic home for originalist constitutional scholarship, led by Randy Barnett.

★ Stanford Constitutional Law Center

Stanford Law’s center on constitutional structure and the separation of powers; positions itself as taking no sides.

★★ The Federalist Society

The most influential organization in the conservative and libertarian legal movement, known for vigorous public debate.

★★ Hoover Institution (Stanford)

Stanford’s public-policy research center; conservative-leaning scholarship across economics, governance, and law.

Books, Video, Podcasts

Worth your time.

Reading, viewing, and listening on the amendment process and constitutional design — kept clear of policy debates, drawn from across the spectrum, and chosen for quality and good-faith argument. Each title is marked with its lean.

Books

★ American Covenant

Yuval Levin, 2024. The Constitution’s structure as the cure for our division rather than its cause — a measured conservative scholar’s argument, praised across the spectrum.

★ Our Republican Constitution

Randy Barnett, 2016. An originalist account of popular sovereignty — “We the People” as individuals rather than “We the States” — from a leading conservative-libertarian scholar.

★ Constitutional Amendments: Making, Breaking, and Changing Constitutions

Richard Albert, 2019. The leading comparative study of how constitutions are amended, broken, and changed; scholarly and nonpartisan.

? Right the Republic

A citizen’s case for eleven structural amendments to repair the machinery of American governance, set apart from the usual policy and culture-war debates. (Written by a Constitution Labs founder. Readers should independently determine political lean if any.)

★ A More Perfect Constitution

Larry Sabato, 2007. A political scientist’s proposal for a constitutional convention and a slate of cross-spectrum amendments to modernize the system.

★ The Living Constitution

David Strauss, 2010. Argues that the Constitution changes mainly through interpretation and practice rather than Article V — the case for “living” constitutionalism.

★ Our Undemocratic Constitution

Sanford Levinson, 2006. A pointed structural critique arguing the Constitution’s design is flawed and calling for a convention to repair it.

★ We the People: A History of the U.S. Constitution

Jill Lepore, 2025. A history of the long, mostly failed effort to amend the Constitution, drawn from the Amendments Project archive; skeptical of originalism.

Documentary & video

★ A More or Less Perfect Union

PBS, 2020. Judge Douglas Ginsburg’s three-part series on the Constitution and its amendments, with conservative, progressive, and libertarian voices in debate.

★ Constitution USA with Peter Sagal

PBS, 2013. An accessible four-part road-trip series on where the Constitution lives, how it works, and where it strains.

Podcasts

★ We the People

National Constitution Center. The Center’s flagship podcast brings left and right scholars together for good-faith debate on constitutional questions; explicitly nonpartisan.

★ Constitutional

The Washington Post. Lillian Cunningham’s narrative-history series on the people and stories behind the Constitution and its amendments.

★ Amarica’s Constitution

Akhil Reed Amar. A leading constitutional scholar walks through the document and current disputes; rigorous, with a discernible liberal cast.

Educational Tools

For the classroom and the curious.

Plain-language material for teachers, students, and the merely curious: a glossary of the terms that trip people up, discussion-ready case studies, and classroom guides by grade level.

Key Terms & Glossary

The words that trip people up — Article V, ratification, supermajority, convention — in plain language.

Article V
The section of the Constitution that sets out how the document is amended.
Amendment
A formal change or addition to the Constitution, carrying the same legal force as the original text.
Proposal
The first stage of amending — formally putting an amendment forward, either by Congress or by a convention of the states.
Ratification
Approval of a proposed amendment by the required number of states, which makes it part of the Constitution.
Supermajority
A threshold higher than a simple majority. Article V requires two-thirds and three-quarters supermajorities at different stages.
Convention of the States
An alternative to congressional proposal, in which a convention called by two-thirds of state legislatures proposes amendments. It has never been carried through to completion.
Ratifying Convention
A special state convention — rather than the legislature — used to vote on ratification. It has been used once, for the 21st Amendment.
Archivist of the United States
The official who certifies and publishes an amendment once three-quarters of the states have ratified it.
Bill of Rights
The first ten amendments, proposed together and ratified in 1791.
Ratification Deadline
A time limit Congress may attach to a proposed amendment, after which an unratified proposal expires.
Repeal
An amendment that cancels an earlier one. The 21st Amendment repealed the 18th — the only instance so far.
Pending Amendment
A proposal that has passed Congress but not yet been ratified by enough states. A few remain technically pending decades later.
